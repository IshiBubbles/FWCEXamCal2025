# Signs of AI Writing — Shared Editing Checklist

Source: Wikipedia's community-maintained guide, "Signs of AI writing" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing). Originally built by Wikipedia editors to catch undisclosed AI-generated content, but the patterns apply to any writing — CVs, personal statements, emails, reports. Use this as a shared checklist for anything drafted in this project, not just the statement coach.

**Important caveat the guide itself makes**: no single sign proves AI authorship, and human writers legitimately use some of these too (em dashes, rule of three, etc.). The value is in catching the *pattern* — several of these stacking up in one piece of writing — not treating any one instance as a violation on its own.

## The core patterns to catch

### 1. Puffery and significance inflation
Phrases that inflate the importance of something without adding real content: "stands as a testament to," "plays a vital/crucial/pivotal role," "underscores the importance of," "a watershed moment," "leaves a lasting impact," "rich tapestry," "deeply rooted." These say something *sounds* significant without saying anything specific.

**Fix**: state the actual fact. If it's genuinely significant, say why in concrete terms — don't just label it significant.

### 2. Fake analysis via "-ing" tails
Sentences that end with a dangling participle pretending to add insight: "...highlighting its importance," "...underscoring its significance," "...reflecting broader trends," "...paving the way for." These add a veneer of analysis without an actual claim behind them.

**Fix**: either make the analytical point a full, specific sentence, or cut the tail entirely.

### 3. Vague attribution / weasel wording
"Some critics argue," "industry reports suggest," "observers have noted," "experts say," "it is widely believed" — invoking authority without naming a real source.

**Fix**: name the actual source, or drop the claim if there isn't one.

### 4. Hollow conclusions and restated summaries
"In conclusion," "In summary," "Overall," "At the end of the day" — followed by a restatement of what was just said, as if the reader can't retain a few paragraphs. Also includes over-explaining obvious points or repeating the same idea in slightly different words.

**Fix**: trust the reader. If a piece doesn't need a summary, don't add one — end on the actual last point.

### 5. Negative parallelisms ("It's not X, it's Y")
A specific contrast-reframe structure AI overuses: "It's not a product launch, it's a movement." Can be legitimate, but overused it reads as manufactured drama rather than genuine contrast.

**Fix**: only use this structure when the contrast is real and earns the emphasis — not as a default rhetorical flourish.

### 6. Rule of three / triplet lists
A repeated pattern of three-item lists, especially in parallel grammatical structure ("fast, flexible, and future-proof"). Adam has already flagged this specifically — it applies to every piece of writing in this project, not just AI-flagged content.

**Fix**: vary list lengths; don't default to three items every time.

### 7. Overly uniform structure / rigid paragraph patterns
Every paragraph starting the same way, identical sentence lengths throughout, or a suspiciously even, symmetrical structure across sections.

**Fix**: vary sentence length and paragraph opening style deliberately.

### 8. Promotional or overly enthusiastic tone where neutral or plain tone is expected
Travel-brochure-style admiration, unearned enthusiasm, or editorializing/opinion inserted into what should be a neutral account.

**Fix**: match tone to context — plain and factual unless there's a real reason for more colour.

### 9. Overused transitions and stock phrasing
Heavy reliance on a small set of connective phrases ("furthermore," "moreover," "in today's world," "it's important to note that") used mechanically rather than because the logic actually calls for them.

**Fix**: cut transition words that aren't doing real logical work.

### 10. Formatting tells
Heavy, inconsistent use of bold text, excessive bullet lists where prose would read better, title-case headings applied unevenly, emoji used as bullet markers.

**Fix**: use formatting only where it earns its place structurally, not as decoration.

### 11. Leftover collaborative phrases
Sentences that read like they were meant for the person prompting the AI, not the end reader: "I hope this helps!", "let me know if you need anything else," "Here's a revised version." A sign a draft was pasted in without a final human pass.

**Fix**: always do a final read specifically for anything that sounds like it's talking to the drafter rather than the reader.

## How to actually use this

For Fraser's personal statement and STAR work specifically, this overlaps heavily with `research.md` — the same red flags (generic trait-claims, unearned significance, clichéd phrasing) show up in both. Use both checklists together during review: `research.md` catches content-level weaknesses (no evidence behind a claim), this file catches surface-level AI-writing tells (phrasing and structure).

For any other output in this project — CVs, cover letters, tracker digests, emails — run a final pass against this list before calling something finished. A quick self-check:

1. Any puffery or "-ing" tail analysis that doesn't say anything concrete? Cut it.
2. Any attribution without a real, named source? Fix or remove.
3. A summary/conclusion that just repeats what came before? Cut it.
4. A rule-of-three list where a different length would read more naturally? Vary it.
5. Suspiciously uniform paragraph or sentence structure throughout? Break the pattern.
6. Anything that sounds like it's addressed to the person who asked for the draft, rather than the actual reader? Remove it.
