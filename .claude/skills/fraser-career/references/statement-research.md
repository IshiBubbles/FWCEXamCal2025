# Personal Statement Research Reference

Distilled from research into UCAS, apprenticeship, and general personal statements — what good ones do, what bad ones do. Use this to inform coaching and drafting decisions; don't recite it at Fraser verbatim.

## The three formats

### UCAS (2026 entry onwards format)
Three separate questions, each min. 350 characters, 4,000 characters total across all three. Reviewed as one whole — no repetition across answers.

- **Q1 — Why this course/subject?** Motivation, subject knowledge, super-curricular activity (reading/podcasts/things done beyond the syllabus), future plans.
- **Q2 — How have qualifications/studies prepared you?** Relevant subjects, transferable skills, achievements. Don't restate grades — they're shown elsewhere on the form.
- **Q3 — What else outside education, and why useful?** Work experience, volunteering, personal responsibilities, hobbies, extracurriculars — reflected on, not just listed.

### Apprenticeship application statement
Different job to UCAS: focused on workplace-relevant transferable skills via volunteering/work experience/extracurriculars, not academic super-curriculars. Employers expect:
- Genuine research into the specific company (products, services, recent news)
- Clear answer to what specifically about *this* apprenticeship appeals — not just the sector
- Evidence mapped to the actual listed criteria/keywords in that employer's ad
- Tailored per employer — a single generic version is a weaker submission everywhere it's used

### General / cover-letter style
Less formulaic. Engaging (non-generic) opening → body evidencing skills/fit with specifics → confident close that adds something rather than just repeating the opening.

## What makes writing strong

- **Show, don't tell.** "I'm a hard worker" proves nothing — reviewers have read it a thousand times. Replace the label with the specific moment that demonstrates it.
- **STAR structure, told naturally.** Situation (brief) → Task → Action (the important part — "I" statements, active voice) → Result (concrete outcome, or genuine reflection if no clean metric exists). Don't label the parts out loud in the final text.
- **Specificity only that person could write.** The test: if you swapped in another candidate's name, would the sentence still work? If yes, it's still a cliché — needs 2–3 concrete details only this person would know.
- **Reflection over listing.** What it meant and how it changed the person matters more than the bare fact of having done it. If it's already on a CV/form elsewhere, the statement's job is to unpack it, not repeat it.
- **An honest complication.** A moment with a real difficulty, doubt, or a mistake corrected reads as more credible than a flawless win narrative.
- **Genuine tailoring.** Evidence of real research into the specific course or employer, not generic enthusiasm that could apply anywhere.

## What makes writing weak — red flags to catch in review

- Generic trait-claims with no backing evidence ("team player," "hard worker," "passionate")
- Passive framing that shrinks the applicant's role ("was involved in," "helped with," "assisted")
- Clichéd openings — "ever since I was young," childhood-dream origin stories, borrowed inspirational quotes
- A chronological CV-in-prose — just re-listing achievements the reader can already see elsewhere, with no reflection
- A conclusion that just restates the opening rather than adding a final thought
- Crossing from personal into confessional — content the person wouldn't be comfortable being asked about face to face
- Using a single dramatic/traumatic event as the *sole* justification for a career choice — reads as saturated and tells the reader about feelings, not behaviour
- Sweeping generalisations without examples — signals either low effort or last-minute writing
- Mismatched writing quality vs. the rest of the application — raises suspicion about how much help was given
- Rule-of-three triplet lists, overly templated phrasing ("thrive at pace," "help drive results") — Adam has flagged these specifically as things to avoid throughout

## Quick self-check before finalising any answer

1. Could this sentence have been written by any other applicant with a similar CV? If yes — cut or add specificity.
2. Is there a claimed trait with no story behind it? Cut the claim, keep only the story.
3. Is the action passive? Rewrite as an active, personal decision.
4. Does the ending add something new, or just repeat what's already been said?
5. Does it match this specific format's actual expectations (see above), not a generic essay style?
